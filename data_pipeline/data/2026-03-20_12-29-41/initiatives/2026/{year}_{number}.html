Stop Funding Russia’s War: Phase Out Harmful and Useless Russian Imports into the EU

Objectives

This initiative calls on the European Commission to propose firm, immediate measures to end the EU’s remaining import dependencies on Russia and Belarus by introducing sectoral bans or decisive phase-outs, not slow or symbolic transitions. Despite years of sanctions, the EU continues to import billions of euros’ worth of strategic goods from Russia, money that directly finances its war and undermines Europe’s security.

We target sectors where viable alternatives already exist within the EU and among trusted partners, such as iron and steel, inorganic chemicals and potassium fertilisers. These imports are replaceable, economically unjustified, and politically indefensible.

The objective is to stop the flow of EU funds to the Russian state, eliminate structural vulnerabilities in critical supply chains and reinforce a coherent, values-based trade policy. Europe cannot claim to support Ukraine while sustaining trade patterns that fuel the aggression. Real security requires real decoupling now.


Annex

What moves this initiative forward is a simple and uncomfortable truth: more than three years into Russia’s full-scale invasion, Ukraine is running out of the resources it needs to defend itself, while the European Union continues to send billions of euros every year to the very state that started this war. These flows are not abstract figures. They are money that fills the Russian budget, sustains its war machine and prolongs an aggression that Europe claims to oppose. And yet, for many of these products, real alternatives exist within our own industrial landscape and in trusted partner countries. The contradiction has become impossible to ignore.

The trade relationship with Russia and Belarus did not collapse with the first sanctions packages. Even now, large segments of our import structure remain untouched. In some sectors, this is the result of outdated exemptions designed for a different political moment. The case of steel slabs is symptomatic: while European and allied producers are fully capable of supplying the market, a long-standing exemption allows these imports to continue until 2028 (at least as it was already extended!). In practice, this carve-out exists almost entirely for the benefit of a single (Russian) company, while the European steel sector struggles under carbon costs and unfair subsidised competition. This is not the kind of economic model Europeans want, nor one that can withstand the geopolitical pressures of our time.

The frustration that inspires this initiative is shared by citizens across Europe who see their governments debating how to finance support for Ukraine while, at the same time, EU import statistics reveal a steady stream of payments to Russia. We are told solidarity requires sacrifice, yet we allow trade patterns that contradict both our values and our security interests. For many people, it feels as though the burden is falling on taxpayers, while simple structural changes like tariffs or the removal of unjustified quotas are left unexplored. The message this sends is one of hesitation when what Europe needs is resolve.

Sectors such as iron and steel, inorganic chemicals and potassium fertilisers illustrate the point clearly. None of these are irreplaceable. For steel, Europe has substantial unused capacity; allied partners can supply what we cannot (for slabs Brazil, Vietnam and many others). For inorganic chemicals like ammonia and calcium phosphates, large global producers including the United States are willing and able to substitute Russian volumes. For potassium fertilisers, the current system of a zero-tariff quota keeps the door open even though imports from Russia are increasing and even though high-quality sources exist in Europe and in friendly countries such as Canada and Jordan. The notion that we must continue buying from Russia to keep European agriculture stable is outdated and disproved by the success of recent diversification in other sectors once deemed impossible to replace.

This initiative therefore urges the Commission to propose a new regulation to phase out remaining exemptions and excessive quotas.

We have alternatives, we have the capacity, and we have the responsibility to act. The war has already cost too much for us to continue financing the aggressor through inertia or convenience. This initiative seeks to end that contradiction and ensure that Europe on the side of its own security and of Ukraine’s survival.
